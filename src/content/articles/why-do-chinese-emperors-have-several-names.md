---
title: Why do Chinese emperors have several names?
description: Personal, reign, temple, and posthumous names answered different political and ritual needs.

---

Chinese emperors can seem to have several “real names” because different names served different purposes. The most important distinction is between a ruler’s personal name, a reign name, a temple name, and a posthumous name.

## Personal name

An emperor had a personal name before accession. Once he ruled, that name could become subject to taboo, so it was not the ordinary way officials and writers referred to him.

## Reign name

A reign name (*nianhao*, 年號) named a period of rule and was used for dating documents, coins, inscriptions, and events. Earlier emperors could change reign names more than once. From the Ming onward, one reign name normally covered one emperor’s reign, which is why English-language histories commonly say the Kangxi or Qianlong emperor.

## Temple name and posthumous name

A temple name (*miaohao*, 廟號) was used in ancestral ritual after an emperor’s death. A posthumous name (*shihao*, 諡號) offered an evaluative title after death. These names belong to ritual and historical memory, not to everyday childhood identity.

For example, the Yongle emperor’s personal name was Zhu Di; Yongle was his reign name; Chengzu was a temple name; and he also had a posthumous title. Each label points to a different setting.

## Reading rule

Never assume that two different emperor names identify two different people. Check the dynasty, dates, and name type first. Conversely, do not use an emperor’s personal name casually when the historical context calls for a reign or temple name.
