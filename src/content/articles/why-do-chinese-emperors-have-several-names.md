## The short answer

**Chinese emperors had several names because the names served different purposes: identifying a person, dating a reign period, placing a deceased ruler in ancestral worship, or commemorating him after death.**

**Zhu Yuanzhang, the Hongwu emperor, and Ming Taizu are the same ruler**, identified respectively by personal name, reign name, and temple name. These are not three successive emperors.

This guide focuses on reading those distinctions, especially in Ming and Qing references. It is not a claim that every ruler in Chinese history had exactly the same set of titles.

## Four name types at a glance

| Type | Chinese term | Main purpose | When it applies | Hongwu example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personal name | 名, *ming*; also encountered as 諱, *hui*, in respectful biographical wording | Identifies the person | May predate accession; imperial names could be subject to taboo | 元璋, Yuanzhang, with family name 朱, Zhu |
| Reign or era name | 年號/年号, *nianhao* | Labels a period used for dating | During the relevant era | 洪武, Hongwu |
| Temple name | 廟號/庙号, *miaohao* | Identifies the deceased ruler in the ancestral-temple system | Assigned after death; historical references also use it | 太祖, Taizu, qualified as Ming Taizu |
| Posthumous name | 諡號/谥号, *shihao* | Commemorative title assessing or praising a deceased person | After death; titles could be enlarged or revised | 高皇帝, Gao Huangdi, the short form used here |

Zhu Yuanzhang's name forms are recorded in the [Palace Museum's Hongwu biography](https://www.dpm.org.cn/court/lineage/226244.html). Its full posthumous titles are much longer than the short form in the table, and the entry records a later revision.

A posthumous title expresses a commemorative judgment, not an independent verdict on a ruler's conduct. The Ministry of Education's [definition of 諡](https://dict.revised.moe.edu.tw/dictView.jsp?ID=8799&la=0&powerMode=0) also makes clear that such titles were not exclusive to emperors.

## Two emperors, several labels

| Name you encounter | Category | Person it identifies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zhu Yuanzhang (朱元璋) | Family name + personal name | The Ming founder |
| Hongwu (洪武), as in “the Hongwu emperor” | Reign name used to identify a ruler | Zhu Yuanzhang |
| Ming Taizu (明太祖) | Dynasty + temple name | Zhu Yuanzhang |
| Gao Huangdi (高皇帝), in this ruler's posthumous title | Short posthumous form; context is essential | Zhu Yuanzhang |
| Zhu Di (朱棣) | Family name + personal name | The Ming ruler associated with the Yongle era |
| Yongle (永樂/永乐) | Reign name | Zhu Di |
| Ming Chengzu (明成祖) | Dynasty + later temple name | Zhu Di |
| Ming Taizong (明太宗), in the relevant earlier record | Dynasty + original temple name | Also Zhu Di, not another Yongle-era ruler |

The [Palace Museum's Yongle biography](https://www.dpm.org.cn/court/lineage/226257.html) records Zhu Di's original temple name Taizong; its [Jiajing chronology](https://www.dpm.org.cn/court/lineage/226241.html) records the change to Chengzu in 1538. **Even a posthumous identification can have a history.** Use the title appropriate to the source, while explaining the identity to a modern reader.

These two Ming cases are useful demonstrations, not a template into which every earlier emperor must fit. Do not identify an otherwise unknown ruler from “Taizu,” “Taizong,” or a short posthumous form without checking dynasty and context.

## Why “reign name” is not simply “the emperor's new personal name”

A *nianhao* labels an era. Modern historians can use it as a convenient label for the ruler, but its calendrical function remains important.

The [Palace Museum's Qianlong biography](https://www.dpm.org.cn/court/lineage/226263.html), for instance, introduces the ruler as Hongli of the Aisin Gioro clan, identifies Qianlong as his era name, and uses Qing Gaozong in its account. A reader seeing Hongli, Qianlong, and Qing Gaozong should therefore check name categories before assuming three people are involved.

In a date such as “Hongwu year 10,” the number counts years of the era. It is not the ruler's age. For exact conversion, use a reliable calendar reference: a Chinese lunar year does not have exactly the same boundaries as a Gregorian calendar year.

An era label on an object is also a different question from who physically made it. Naming the reign associated with an inscription does not turn the emperor into its artisan.

## Did every emperor use just one reign name?

**No.** One era name for a ruler's reign is a useful general pattern in Ming and Qing study, not an unbroken rule for all imperial history. Earlier rulers could use multiple era names; David K. Jordan's [“Imperial Names” overview](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/chin/ChNames.html) explains why era labels are particularly useful in later imperial references.

There is an important Ming exception worth learning rather than hiding:

- Zhu Qizhen first ruled under the **Zhengtong (正統/正统)** era.
- After capture and displacement, he later returned to the throne and used **Tianshun (天順/天顺)**.
- **Ming Yingzong (明英宗)** is the temple name linking those two periods to the same man.

The [Palace Museum's Tianshun biography](https://www.dpm.org.cn/court/lineage/226253.html) records both phases. Two era names do not necessarily mean two emperors, just as two imperial names do not necessarily mean two people.

## Were these names used in conversation?

Do not turn a modern book's identification into a reconstructed line of court dialogue.

- A historian may call a ruler by a temple name assigned after his death. That label is not evidence of how a minister addressed him while he lived.
- A reign label can date a document without being a spoken form of address.
- Personal-name taboos help explain why using an emperor's given name was not a neutral choice in every historical setting.
- To reconstruct an audience, letter, or memorial, consult examples from that period rather than replacing every name with one English honorific.

This is a distinction between **identifying someone for a reader** and **addressing that person in a particular relationship**. For related naming vocabulary outside imperial titles, see [ming, zi, and hao explained](/names/personal-names-courtesy-names-and-style-names/).

## Quick questions

### Is “Ming” the Hongwu emperor's surname?

No. Ming is the dynasty label in “Ming Taizu”; Zhu is the family name in Zhu Yuanzhang. Do not treat dynasty + temple name as an ordinary family-name + given-name formula.

### Is a temple name a courtesy name?

No. A courtesy name is a *zi* (字), not a *miaohao* (廟號). “Name used instead of a personal name” is too broad a definition to distinguish the categories.

### Does “posthumous name” mean the same thing as “temple name”?

No. Both concern the deceased ruler, but they have different functions. The Hongwu example distinguishes Taizu from the short posthumous form Gao Huangdi. They may appear together in a longer designation without becoming the same category.

### Must I use the same naming convention in every dynasty?

Use a reliable reference's established identification and define alternatives on first mention. For example: “Zhu Yuanzhang, the Hongwu emperor (Ming Taizu).” Then remain consistent unless the change of label is itself relevant.

## A practical reading checklist

1. **Identify the dynasty and dates** before matching similar-looking labels.
2. **Ask what the label is doing:** identifying a person, dating an era, or commemorating a deceased ruler?
3. **Check the Chinese category** where available: 年號, 廟號, and 諡號 are not interchangeable.
4. **Look for changes and interrupted reigns**, as with Zhu Di's temple designation or Zhu Qizhen's two era names.
5. **Keep narrative labels separate from historical dialogue.**
