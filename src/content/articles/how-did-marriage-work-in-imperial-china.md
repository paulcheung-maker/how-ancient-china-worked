## The short answer

In much of imperial China, marriage joined households as well as two individuals. Families often played the central role in arranging a match, and a bride commonly moved into or near her husband's family after marriage. But practices changed across dynasties, regions, social classes, and communities. Marriage was never a single fixed system.

The ideal presented in legal and moral texts was only one part of the story. Household records, contracts, biographies, and local evidence show negotiation, remarriage, divorce, widowhood, adoption, and many forms of family strategy.

## A marriage was a family alliance

Marriage could create kinship ties, secure labor and heirs, manage property, or improve a family's standing. Matchmakers and exchanges of gifts were common features in many periods. Families considered reputation, resources, horoscope practices in some settings, and the practical needs of both households.

Parents and senior kin therefore often had substantial influence. That does not prove that every bride or groom had no voice. The degree of choice varied by family, locality, wealth, and period, and the surviving sources often record elite ideals more readily than ordinary people's decisions.

## Residence after marriage

In many Han and later imperial contexts, the usual pattern was patrilocal: a woman joined her husband's household. This made the husband's family important in her daily life, work, and status. A new bride might live with parents-in-law, siblings-in-law, and other relatives rather than in a separate nuclear household.

Other arrangements existed. Economic need, inheritance, migration, widowhood, and local custom could lead to different residence patterns. Historical terms such as “Chinese marriage” conceal these differences.

## Gifts, dowries, and property

Gifts exchanged during marriage could have economic and symbolic purposes. A bride's family might provide a dowry; the groom's side might give betrothal or marriage gifts. The names, legal treatment, and relative importance of these transfers changed over time.

Dowry did not automatically mean that a woman independently controlled all property, and bridewealth did not automatically mean “buying” a wife. Both were embedded in kinship, law, ritual, and household finance. The details must be read in their time and place.

## Concubinage and social rank

Some wealthy men kept concubines, but this was not the everyday experience of all households. It depended on resources and was shaped by law and custom. The principal wife, concubines, children, and senior kin could have different statuses within the household; these distinctions affected inheritance and ritual.

It is important not to treat elite households as typical. Most families had fewer resources and faced more immediate concerns: land, work, food, survival, and children.

## Divorce, remarriage, and widowhood

Imperial legal systems recognized forms of divorce, and social expectations could be stricter than legal possibility. A husband’s ability to repudiate a wife, the protections available to a wife, the treatment of remarriage, and expectations of widow chastity all shifted across the centuries.

Women and men did remarry. Widows might head households, return to natal kin, remarry, or preserve a family line in other ways. The historical record contains real constraints, but it also contains people adapting rules to difficult circumstances.

## What marriage helps explain

Marriage shaped names, inheritance, household membership, and relationships with the state. It is one reason a simple question about a woman's surname or a household register needs a broader picture of family life.
