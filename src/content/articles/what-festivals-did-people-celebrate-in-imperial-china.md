## The short answer

People in imperial China marked the lunar calendar, agricultural seasons, family obligations, religious observances, and local traditions through festivals. The best-known were the New Year season, Lantern Festival, Qingming, Dragon Boat Festival, Mid-Autumn Festival, and Double Ninth. But the meaning and scale of each celebration differed by dynasty, region, household, religion, and social class.

A festival could mean reunion and food for one family, temple processions for another, and a commercial opportunity for a city.

## New Year was more than one day

The lunar New Year was the most important annual transition for many households. Preparations could include cleaning, settling debts, buying or making food, honoring ancestors, exchanging greetings, and avoiding unlucky actions at the start of the year. These practices varied, but the underlying idea of renewal and family continuity was widespread.

For households with little money, the holiday could also expose economic pressure: food, gifts, ritual offerings, and travel all cost resources.

## Seasonal festivals connected people to time

Qingming combined seasonal change with tomb visiting and remembrance of ancestors. The Dragon Boat Festival was associated in many places with seasonal customs, food, racing, and later stories of Qu Yuan. Mid-Autumn brought moon viewing and family-oriented celebration. Double Ninth had associations with autumn, climbing, and respect for elders in some traditions.

These familiar names should not be treated as unchanging packages. Stories attached to a festival, foods eaten, and popular activities developed over centuries.

## Religion and local life mattered

Buddhist, Daoist, and local temple calendars created additional occasions for offerings, performances, fairs, and processions. Local deities, saints, ancestors, guilds, and lineages could each have their own dates. A traveler moving from one county to another might find a different festival calendar and different public rituals.

State ritual was another layer. Courts staged ceremonies for the calendar, harvest, ancestors, and imperial authority; ordinary people did not simply copy court practice.

## Festivals created markets too

Celebrations generated demand for food, candles, paper goods, decorations, clothing, entertainment, and travel. Marketplaces and temple fairs could become meeting points where commerce and ritual overlapped. This is one reason festivals are useful for understanding both household life and the wider economy.
