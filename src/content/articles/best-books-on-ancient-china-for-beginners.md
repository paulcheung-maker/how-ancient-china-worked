If you want one dependable place to start, choose **Patricia Buckley Ebrey's _The Cambridge Illustrated History of China_**. It gives a clear chronological overview, strong visual context, and enough political and social history to make later reading easier.

The best book depends on what you mean by “ancient China.” Some readers want the Qin and Han empires; others mean the whole imperial period up to 1911. This list starts broad, then offers focused next steps.

## The short list

### 1. _The Cambridge Illustrated History of China_ — Patricia Buckley Ebrey

**Best first book for most beginners.**

Ebrey provides a readable survey from early China through the twentieth century. The illustrations, maps, and short sections make it especially useful if you are still learning the dynasties and their order.

Choose this if you want the broad picture before specializing.

### 2. _China: A History_ — John Keay

**Best for readers who prefer a continuous story.**

Keay's single-volume narrative moves briskly across a long period. It is a good companion to an illustrated survey: use it when you want the sequence of events and changing dynasties to feel like one connected history.

Choose this if you enjoy narrative history more than textbook-style reference.

### 3. _The Open Empire: A History of China to 1600_ — Valerie Hansen

**Best for premodern China beyond emperors and battles.**

Hansen pays close attention to trade, religion, technology, art, and contacts with the wider world. It is an excellent second book once you know the basic dynastic outline.

Choose this if you want to understand how ordinary life and exchange shaped the empire.

### 4. _The Early Chinese Empires: Qin and Han_ — Mark Edward Lewis

**Best focused introduction to the first imperial empires.**

This volume explains how the Qin and Han created durable imperial institutions, including administration, law, military power, and ideas of rulership. It pairs well with our guide to [government in imperial China](/government/).

Choose this if your main question is how the imperial system began.

### 5. _Imperial China 900–1800_ — F. W. Mote

**Best next step for serious readers.**

Mote is more demanding than the first three choices, but richly rewarding. It is strongest once you already have a sense of the Song, Yuan, Ming, and Qing periods.

Choose this if you want a deeper account of the later imperial world.

## Which book should you buy first?

Use this simple rule:

- Want a clear overview with maps and images? Start with **Ebrey**.
- Want a fast, connected narrative? Start with **Keay**.
- Most interested in the premodern world before 1600? Start with **Hansen**.
- Interested in the Qin and Han specifically? Start with **Lewis**.
- Already know the basics and want depth? Choose **Mote**.

Buying one good overview and reading it is more useful than collecting five books at once.

## A sensible reading order

1. Start with Ebrey or Keay.
2. Read Hansen to see how society, exchange, and culture fit into the political story.
3. Pick one era for deeper study, such as Qin–Han or Song–Qing.
4. Use topic guides alongside your reading—for example, [what the civil-service examination was](/government/what-was-the-civil-service-examination/) or [how personal, courtesy, and style names differed](/names/personal-names-courtesy-names-and-style-names/).

## Before you choose a book

Check the date range in the title or description. “Ancient China” is used loosely in popular writing: some books stop around the Han dynasty, while others cover the whole imperial period. A beginner's first book should make its scope clear and should include maps, a timeline, and a bibliography for further reading.
