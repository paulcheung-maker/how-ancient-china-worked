## The short answer

**Yes. Women could have courtesy names (*zi*, 字).** Early ritual texts prescribe a woman's *zi* in connection with betrothal, and historical biographies explicitly record women's *zi*. Courtesy names were therefore not exclusively male. This does **not** establish that every woman in every dynasty had one.

The key is to distinguish a ritual ideal, a name recorded for an individual, and the frequency of a practice. Each requires different evidence.

## What was a courtesy name?

A courtesy name was an additional name associated with adult social identity, not a family name. “Style name” can also mean *zi* in English, although translators sometimes use “style” more loosely. It is not automatically a pen name.

For the broader vocabulary, see [personal names, courtesy names, and style names](/names/personal-names-courtesy-names-and-style-names/).

## What do the ritual texts say?

The *Book of Rites* (*Liji*), in “Qu Li I,” gives two nearby prescriptions: a man receives a *zi* at capping at twenty, and a betrothed woman receives one at the hairpin ceremony. The phrase for women is **女子許嫁，笄而字**. In plain English: when a woman is betrothed, she undergoes the hairpin rite and receives a courtesy name. This is our paraphrase, not a quotation from an English translation. [Read the Chinese text](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/禮記/曲禮上).

The passage places female courtesy names within a ritual framework. However, a prescriptive text describes what its authors considered proper; it is not a census of actual practices across later imperial society.

“Women receive a courtesy name upon betrothal in this prescription” is consequently more defensible than “all women received one at a fixed age.”

## Recorded examples: Ban Zhao and Huan Shaojun

The *Book of the Later Han* (*Hou Hanshu*), chapter 84, supplies concrete examples:

| Person | What the linked text records | What it demonstrates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ban Zhao (班昭) | Personal name Zhao; *zi* Huiban (惠班) in this transmitted text | A biography distinguishes a woman's personal name and *zi* |
| Huan Shaojun (桓少君), wife of Bao Xuan | Huan family name; *zi* Shaojun (少君) | A familiar historical label can preserve a courtesy name rather than a personal name |

Both appear in [chapter 84](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/後漢書/卷84). Ban Zhao's entry also introduces her through her husband and father. A relationship-based identification and a recorded courtesy name can therefore coexist.

These examples come from a transmitted historical work about Han people, not surviving name-registration forms. They establish the existence of female *zi*, not a rate of use among all women. For a particular spelling, consult the relevant edition rather than assuming all modern references reproduce an identical text.

## Why are women often called “Lady X”?

A writer's chosen label need not be a person's complete set of names. A family name, relationship, or title may be sufficient for that account.

The practical rule is: **“not recorded here” does not mean “never existed.”** Conversely, an elegant name in a modern retelling is not automatically a documented courtesy name.

Treat “Lady Zhang,” for example, as a hypothetical family-based designation unless the source gives more information. Do not invent a personal name or *zi* to fill a gap in the record. A related but separate question is [whether women took their husbands' surnames](/names/did-women-take-their-husbands-surnames/).

## How to check a woman's name

1. **Find the relevant historical account.** A fictional adaptation is not evidence for a real person's name.
2. **Look for a naming formula.** *Ming* (名) identifies a personal name; *zi* (字), used as a naming label, identifies a courtesy name. *Hao* (號) requires its own context.
3. **Separate the source from your inference.** If an epitaph supplies only a family name, report that limitation.
4. **Keep the period and social setting attached.** An elite Han biography cannot establish a universal rule for ordinary women in Ming or Qing villages.

For fiction, a female courtesy name can be plausible, but plausibility is not proof that a real individual used it. Label invented names as fictional.

## Common questions

### Did every woman have a courtesy name?

These sources do not establish that. The prescription and named examples show that the practice existed, not that it was universal.

### Is a courtesy name the same as a pen name?

Not necessarily. Check the original term and context instead of merging courtesy names, literary aliases, surnames, and titles.

### Does a missing courtesy name prove someone had none?

No. It tells you that the source you checked does not supply one. A stronger conclusion needs additional evidence.
