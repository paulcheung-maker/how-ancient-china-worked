The short answer is: sometimes, but not in the same routine, well-documented way as elite men.

In imperial China, a **courtesy name** (*zi*) was commonly adopted by educated men as they entered adulthood. It was used by peers and in formal writing, while a personal name was reserved for family elders and intimate settings. Women’s naming practices were different: surviving records often identify them by family name, marital status, kinship role, or a posthumous title rather than by a personal or courtesy name.

## What the evidence shows

Some women—especially in literate, elite families—did have personal names and, on occasion, courtesy names. Poetry collections, epitaphs, genealogies, and biographical writing preserve examples. Yet these examples should not be treated as proof of a universal convention. The sources themselves are uneven: women’s names were often omitted or recorded differently from men’s.

## Why the distinction matters

It is tempting to give every historical woman a courtesy name when writing fiction or family histories. A more accurate default is to ask three questions:

1. What period and social setting are we looking at?
2. What kind of source records the person?
3. Does that source actually use a *zi*, or another naming form?

For a first pass, use the terms in the source and describe uncertainty plainly. Naming was a social practice, not a fixed rule applied equally to everyone.
