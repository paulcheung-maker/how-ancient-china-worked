title: Did women take their husbands’ surnames in imperial China?
description: Marriage changed a woman’s household position, but the names used in records were more complicated than a simple surname replacement.
publishedAt: 2026-10-02
section: Names
---

Not in the straightforward modern sense of permanently replacing a natal surname with a husband’s surname.

In many imperial Chinese records, a married woman is identified by her natal surname plus a marker such as *shi* (氏), or by a combination that relates her to her husband’s household. A source may call her “the Wang woman,” “Madam Li,” “wife of Zhang,” or identify her through a son or father. Each form tells us something about the writer’s purpose, not necessarily the exact name she used every day.

## Why the records can be confusing

Historical writing often gave women less personal-name detail than men. Genealogies, epitaphs, legal records, and fiction used different conventions. A woman could retain significance through her natal kin while living in her husband’s household; a record might emphasize either connection.

This means that an English translation such as “Mrs. Zhang” can be convenient, but it may hide the original wording. It does not automatically mean she legally changed from Wang to Zhang.

## Marriage and identity

Marriage usually changed residence, obligations, and household affiliation. It did not erase natal kinship. In late imperial lineage society, both natal and marital connections could matter, particularly in marriage negotiations, inheritance, mourning, and remembrance.

The safest practice is to state the Chinese form when it matters: for example, “a woman of the Wang surname, married into the Zhang household.” That is clearer than forcing the evidence into a modern first-name/last-name model.
