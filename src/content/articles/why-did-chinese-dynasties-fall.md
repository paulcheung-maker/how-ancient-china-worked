## The short answer

Chinese dynasties did not fall because of one predictable cause. Rebellions, court conflict, fiscal strain, military weakness, environmental crisis, disease, regional power, and foreign conquest could combine in different ways. The familiar “dynastic cycle” is a useful teaching model, but it is not a machine that automatically explains every collapse.

The key question is how multiple pressures weakened a government's ability to raise revenue, feed people, command armies, and maintain legitimacy.

## Common pressures

A dynasty could face poor harvests, floods, droughts, rising prices, population movement, or epidemics. These problems became political crises when relief failed, taxes remained heavy, or local order broke down.

Military pressure was equally important. Frontier wars and internal rebellions were expensive. If commanders became autonomous or soldiers went unpaid, the state could lose control rapidly.

## Court and fiscal problems

Succession disputes, factional conflict, corruption, and weak leadership could damage a government, but they rarely acted alone. A court divided by politics might struggle to respond to famine or invasion; a treasury under strain might cut military readiness or relief.

Tax systems also had limits. When powerful groups avoided burdens or records became inaccurate, the state could not easily collect what it needed.

## Conquest and replacement

Some dynasties were overthrown by groups from beyond their previous borders, including the Mongols and Manchus. These were not outside Chinese history; conquest dynasties governed China, adopted institutions selectively, and shaped later political traditions.

A new regime needed more than victory. It had to secure revenue, win cooperation from local elites, feed cities and armies, and present itself as legitimate.

## The Mandate of Heaven afterward

After a collapse, historians often described the old dynasty as having lost the Mandate of Heaven. This gave moral meaning to disaster and conquest, but it should not replace concrete explanation. The mandate was the language used to interpret change, not the sole cause of change.
