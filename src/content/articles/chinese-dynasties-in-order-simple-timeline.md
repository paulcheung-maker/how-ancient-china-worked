## The short answer

The main dynastic sequence is **Xia → Shang → Zhou → Qin → Han → Jin → Sui → Tang → Song → Yuan → Ming → Qing**. This memory aid leaves out periods of divided rule; the fuller timeline below includes them. A dynasty is a ruling house, not a guarantee that it controlled all of modern China.

The simplest way to remember Chinese history is not as one unbroken empire, but as a sequence of states that repeatedly unified, divided, and reunified a vast region.

This timeline gives the major periods in order. Dates are approximate for the earliest eras, and several entries are periods of division rather than a single dynasty.

## Chinese dynasties at a glance

1. **Xia** (traditionally c. 2070–1600 BCE)
2. **Shang** (c. 1600–1046 BCE)
3. **Zhou** (1046–256 BCE)
4. **Qin** (221–206 BCE)
5. **Han** (206 BCE–220 CE)
6. **Three Kingdoms** (220–280)
7. **Jin** (266–420)
8. **Northern and Southern Dynasties** (420–589)
9. **Sui** (581–618)
10. **Tang** (618–907)
11. **Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms** (907–979)
12. **Song** (960–1279)
13. **Yuan** (1271–1368)
14. **Ming** (1368–1644)
15. **Qing** (1644–1912)

## Why do the dates overlap?

Jin began in 266 while Wu, one of the Three Kingdoms, survived until 280. Sui began while the southern Chen state still existed. Song and Yuan overlapped before the Mongol conquest was complete.

There are also **two different Jin dynasties**: the Jin of 266–420 (晉) and the Jurchen Jin of 1115–1234 (金). The latter ruled northern China alongside Southern Song. Liao and Western Xia also shaped that period. Omitting them from a memory chain does not mean they were unimportant. The Met's [parallel Song, Liao, Jin, and Yuan chronology](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/07/eac.html) helps explain this.

## Before the empires: Xia, Shang, and Zhou

### Xia

The Xia is traditionally placed first in Chinese dynastic histories. Archaeology has made early Bronze Age societies far clearer, but scholars still debate how directly particular archaeological cultures can be identified with the Xia of later texts. It is best treated as the beginning of the traditional dynastic sequence, not as a fully documented empire in the later sense.

### Shang

The Shang is the first dynasty supported by substantial contemporary written evidence. Oracle-bone inscriptions record divination, royal rituals, warfare, and the Shang court. Its political center was in the Yellow River region.

### Zhou

The Zhou overthrew the Shang and ruled for a very long period. The early Western Zhou established ideas about kingship and political legitimacy that later writers described through the “Mandate of Heaven.”

The later Eastern Zhou was far less unified. Its final centuries included the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, when many competing states fought, made alliances, and developed new institutions.

## The first unified empire: Qin and Han

### Qin

The Qin state conquered its rivals and unified much of what is now China in 221 BCE. Its empire was brief, but its administrative experiments, standardization efforts, and imperial title had enormous influence.

### Han

The Han lasted more than four centuries with a short interruption. It is commonly divided into Western Han and Eastern Han. Han governments developed durable practices of taxation, registration, court politics, and local administration.

For a closer look at how later imperial governments worked, read [What Did a County Magistrate Do?](/government/what-did-a-county-magistrate-do/).

## Division and reunification: Three Kingdoms to Sui

After the Han collapsed, China was not governed by one lasting unified dynasty for several centuries.

The **Three Kingdoms**—Wei, Shu, and Wu—became famous in later storytelling, but they were followed by further political change. The **Jin** reunited much of the realm briefly before conflict and migration reshaped the north and south.

The phrase **Northern and Southern Dynasties** describes this longer age of divided rule. It was also a period of major cultural exchange, the spread of Buddhism, and economic growth in the south.

The **Sui** was founded in 581 and completed north–south reunification by conquering Chen in 589. The Met's [sixth-century chronology](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/06/eac.html) distinguishes these milestones. Like the Qin, it did not last long, but it created institutions and infrastructure that the Tang inherited.

## The great imperial centuries: Tang and Song

### Tang

The Tang is often remembered for its capital at Chang'an, cosmopolitan connections, poetry, and military reach. It was powerful, but its later decades saw regional military governors gain influence and the central court weaken.

### Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms

After the Tang fell, northern regimes succeeded one another while several states ruled in the south. The name describes a period, not one empire.

### Song

The Song was founded in 960 and consolidated much of China over the following decades, rather than completing reunification on its founding day. It faced strong neighboring states. Its era is associated with commercial growth, urban life, printing, civil administration, and the examination system.

The Song did not control every region once held by the Tang. “Chinese dynasty” should not be read as meaning that the territory of modern China always had the same borders.

## Conquest dynasties and the last empire: Yuan, Ming, and Qing

### Yuan

The Mongol ruler Kublai Khan founded the Yuan dynasty in 1271. It completed the conquest of Southern Song in 1279. Yuan was part of the wider Mongol imperial world, not a name for every Mongol-ruled territory. It connected China to Eurasian networks in new ways.

### Ming

The Ming replaced the Yuan in 1368. It is known for its strong early emperors, an expanding civil bureaucracy, maritime voyages in the early fifteenth century, and later fiscal and military pressures.

### Qing

The Manchu rulers adopted the name Qing in 1636 and took Beijing in 1644, the conventional starting point in many introductory timelines. It became the last imperial dynasty, ruling until the 1911 Revolution brought the imperial system to an end in 1912.

## Common timeline questions

### Was Xia definitely the first dynasty?

Xia is first in the traditional account, but connecting later written accounts to archaeological cultures remains debated. The Met's [Bronze Age timeline](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/03/eac.html) labels Xia unconfirmed. Traditional Xia dates do not have the same certainty as later imperial dates.

### Which dynasty created the first unified empire?

Qin, in 221 BCE. Earlier states existed, but this imperial unification is the key starting point for the imperial sequence. See the Met's [Qin and Han chronology](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/04/eac.html).

### Why do short lists skip from Han to Sui?

They omit centuries of divided rule. Three Kingdoms, Jin, and the Northern and Southern Dynasties belong between them. The main table includes these periods so the sequence does not imply continuous unified rule.

### What was the last dynasty?

Qing. The imperial system ended with abdication in 1912. For the difference between a dynasty ending and the imperial system ending, see [Why did Chinese dynasties fall?](/government/why-did-chinese-dynasties-fall/).

## A better way to learn the sequence

Do not try to memorize every date at once. Start with five anchors:

1. **Zhou** — long early age of kings and competing states.
2. **Qin and Han** — formation of the early empire.
3. **Tang and Song** — major eras of culture, administration, and commerce.
4. **Yuan** — Mongol rule.
5. **Ming and Qing** — the final imperial centuries.

Once these anchors feel familiar, add the periods of division between them. A timeline is a map for further questions, not the whole history.

## What this timeline leaves out

This page lists the main sequence used in general introductions. It does not list every regional state, short-lived regime, or non-Han polity. That omission matters: many different peoples, languages, and political centers shaped the history of China.
