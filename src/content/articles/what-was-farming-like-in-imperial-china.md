## The short answer

For most people in imperial China, farming was not simply an occupation. It organized the year, the household, food supply, tax obligations, and survival. Families planted, weeded, irrigated, harvested, stored, repaired tools, paid dues, and managed risk in a world where a flood, drought, pest outbreak, or illness could undo a season's work.

There was no one “Chinese farm.” Rice-growing regions, dry northern grain fields, upland communities, frontier settlements, and commercial crop areas all followed different calendars and techniques.

## The farming year followed seasons

Spring brought soil preparation, planting, repairs, and decisions about seed. Summer required weeding, water management, and constant attention to crops. Autumn concentrated labor into harvest, threshing, storage, and the settlement of rents or taxes. Winter could mean repairing houses and tools, processing textiles, carrying goods, preparing manure, and planning for the next season.

Agricultural knowledge came through families, neighbors, local custom, and written manuals. It included when to plant, how to use water, which fields needed particular crops, and how to store grain safely.

## Rice, wheat, millet, and more

In much of the south, wet-rice cultivation could produce high yields where water and labor were available. In many northern regions, millet and wheat were important staples. Beans, vegetables, cotton, tea, mulberry for silkworms, and many other crops also shaped local economies.

Crop choice was not determined by climate alone. Markets, rent demands, taxes, household labor, roads, and state policies could all change what families planted.

## Households worked together

Farming involved women, men, children, older relatives, hired laborers, and neighbors in different ways. The exact division of labor changed with crop, season, wealth, and region. Textile work, food preparation, animal care, transplanting, harvesting, and market exchange could all be essential to a family's survival.

Not all cultivators owned the land they worked. Some were owner-farmers; others rented land, worked for large households, or combined cultivation with crafts and trade. The word “peasant” hides these differences.

## Risk and the state

A good harvest did not guarantee security. Grain could be lost to weather, warfare, theft, debt, or transport problems. Taxes and labor obligations made household registration important to governments, while granaries, relief, and irrigation works were recurring state concerns.

Officials often described agriculture as the foundation of the realm. For farming families, it was also a continuous negotiation between household needs and outside demands.
