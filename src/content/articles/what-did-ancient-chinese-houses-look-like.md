## The short answer

Ancient Chinese houses did not all look like the familiar walled courtyard compounds seen in photographs of Beijing. Many homes were made chiefly from **earth, timber, thatch, reeds, or tile**, using materials available nearby. Courtyard layouts were important in many towns and elite households, but the size, finish, and number of rooms varied dramatically.

A house was also a working household space: a place to cook, store grain, make goods, host kin, and manage authority inside the family.

## What archaeology can show

Most ordinary houses were built from perishable materials, so they rarely survive intact. Archaeologists instead find foundations, postholes, rammed-earth walls, hearths, drains, pottery, storage pits, and traces of roofs. At ancient urban sites such as Anyang, these remains show neighborhoods of workshops, storage, ritual spaces, and residences rather than a single uniform city plan.

Rammed earth could form substantial walls and platforms. Timber frames supported roofs. Roof coverings ranged from thatch and other plant materials to tiles, which cost more and required more labor. A grand building visible in a tomb image or excavation is therefore not a safe model for every household.

## Courtyards were useful, not just beautiful

A courtyard organized light, air, work, and movement. Rooms could face inward around a shared open space; gates controlled the transition between street and household. This arrangement was especially suited to multi-generational families and to a social world in which household boundaries mattered.

But courtyard housing came in many scales. An affluent family might have several connected courts, reception rooms, service areas, and storage. A modest house might have a small yard and a few rooms. Rural homes could be quite different again, shaped by farm work, climate, and local building traditions.

## Homes reflected hierarchy

Building materials and decoration could signal status. Tiles, painted surfaces, large gates, raised platforms, and formal reception rooms were easier for wealthy households to obtain. Regulations in some dynasties also tried to distinguish ranks through architecture, clothing, vehicles, and ritual.

That does not mean every house was neatly controlled by law. Wealth, local custom, disasters, repair, and the rapid growth of cities all produced variation. Families also moved, rented, shared compounds, and adapted buildings over time.

## The house and the family

The built space supported family routines. Kitchens and hearths prepared food; wells, jars, and pits stored water and grain; rooms separated sleeping, receiving visitors, and household work to different degrees. Ancestor rites could connect family memory to the domestic compound. In many contexts, senior members of the household exercised authority over younger kin within that space.

Understanding homes helps explain why marriage, inheritance, household registration, and taxation mattered so much: the state and families both treated the household as a crucial unit.

## Do not reduce every home to a siheyuan

The Beijing *siheyuan* is a striking and influential form, but it belongs to specific regions and periods. It should not stand in for all of ancient or imperial China. A better question is: what materials, climate, occupation, and family arrangement shaped this particular home?
