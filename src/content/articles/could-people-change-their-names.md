---
title: Could people change their names in imperial China?
description: Yes. Additional names, new appellations, taboos, and major life changes could all complicate a person’s naming history.
publishedAt: 2026-10-02
section: Names
---

Yes, but “changing a name” can mean several different things.

A person might acquire a courtesy name (*zi*) at adulthood, adopt one or more appellations (*hao*) later in life, receive a religious name, or become known by an office, place, or literary sobriquet. None of these necessarily erased the personal name.

## Addition is not always replacement

The most common mistake is to treat every later name as a legal replacement. In many cases, names accumulated. A scholar might keep the same surname and personal name while signing poems with an appellation. A Buddhist or Daoist practitioner might use a religious name in a different social setting. An official could be recognized by office or place.

Imperial name taboos could also lead to substitutions in writing. That tells us about etiquette and power; it does not always mean an individual formally adopted a new identity.

## Read the context first

Ask what sort of source you are reading. A genealogy may privilege descent names; an examination record may use a formal personal name; a literary collection may foreground an appellation; a court document may avoid a taboo character. Different labels can refer to the same person without contradiction.

For a biography, list the original terms and explain when each was used. For fiction, do not give a character several names merely because historical figures sometimes had them. Choose the form appropriate to the relationship and scene.
