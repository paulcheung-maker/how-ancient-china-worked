## The short answer

Silk mattered in ancient and imperial China because it was more than a luxury cloth. It required specialized knowledge, supported households and workshops, served as a high-value gift and tax payment, marked status, and became one of China's best-known exports.

Its importance changed by place and period. Not everyone wore silk, produced it, or profited from it equally.

## Making silk required skill

Silk comes from the cocoons of silkworms, which are raised on mulberry leaves. Turning cocoons into thread and then cloth requires careful timing, labor, tools, and knowledge. Sericulture could be organized within households, by estates, or in specialized workshops.

Women's labor was often central to spinning and weaving, though the organization of work varied. Imperial states and elite households took an interest in silk because its quality and value made it useful for both practical and ceremonial purposes.

## A form of wealth

Fine silk was light, valuable, and portable. It could be used for clothing, banners, paintings, books, ritual items, gifts, and payments. In some periods, cloth and silk were accepted or required in taxation and official compensation alongside grain or coins.

Silk also made hierarchy visible. The material, color, pattern, and amount of decoration available to an individual could reflect wealth, office, court rules, and local fashion.

## Silk and long-distance exchange

Chinese silks traveled widely through networks now called the Silk Roads. But the name can mislead: those routes carried many goods, people, religions, artistic styles, and technologies in both directions. Silk was important enough to give the network its modern name, yet it was never the only product moving.

The movement of silk connected producers in China with merchants, caravan routes, frontier communities, ports, and consumers far away.

## Not a single Chinese secret

Stories often say that China guarded the “secret” of silk. It is true that sericulture knowledge was valuable, but technological knowledge and material goods move through people, gifts, trade, migration, and political contact. Over time, silk production spread beyond China, while Chinese producers continued to develop distinctive textiles and markets.
