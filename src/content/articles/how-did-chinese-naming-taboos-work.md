A naming taboo (*hui*, 諱) was a rule of avoidance: certain personal names were treated as too charged with respect to use freely. The best-known cases concerned emperors and ancestors.

## What was avoided?

The personal name (*ming*) of a ruler could be avoided in official writing and formal speech. Writers might substitute another character, omit a stroke, leave a blank, or rephrase a word that contained the taboo character. The exact practice varied by dynasty, document type, and level of formality.

Ancestral names mattered too. Within a family, a descendant was expected to avoid using the personal names of senior relatives. This was part of a broader etiquette in which names marked hierarchy and respect.

## Why it mattered

Taboos made language visibly political. A date, place name, book title, or ordinary phrase might need rewriting because it happened to contain an imperial personal-name character. For people writing memorials or working at court, getting it wrong could have serious consequences.

But the rule was never mechanical. Homophones, compound names, handwriting conventions, local usage, and changing dynastic policies all affected how strictly a taboo was applied. A surviving text may therefore preserve an altered character, while another source uses the original.

## How to read a source

If a character seems oddly replaced or abbreviated, ask whether it could reflect a name taboo. Then identify the ruler, ancestor, and date of the text before drawing conclusions. Avoid treating every spelling change as political censorship.

The important point is simple: a personal name was not merely private information. In imperial China it could organize relations of family, authority, and historical memory.
